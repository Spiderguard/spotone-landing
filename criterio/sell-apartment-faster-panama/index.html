Your listing chooses who writes to you

What you put in a listing decides which conversation starts, with whom, and how quickly it gets to an offer.

Message me on WhatsApp

To tell whether a listing is working, don't count the messages. Look at how the first one starts.

When the listing is thin, that message is usually "best price?". When it's complete, the questions sound different: "Does the maintenance fee include water?" or "Can I see it Saturday?". The first person is often still shopping on price. The second has read the listing and wants to know whether the unit fits.

How do you sell an apartment faster in Panama?

Selling an apartment faster in Panama usually depends less on how many people ask than on who asks. A complete listing, with the facts, the drawback stated up front and a price you can explain, draws buyers who already know what they want. You get fewer messages, but more of them can turn into viewings and offers.

The listing screens before you do

By the time you answer the first message, the listing has already done some sorting. Someone who writes after reading a complete listing knows what they're looking for and arrives with a specific question. If the place fits, the conversation moves. If it doesn't, they can tell from the listing itself and don't ask for a viewing that was never going to end in an offer.

A weak listing does the opposite. The messages pile up and you do the screening yourself, one call and one viewing at a time.

Why screening shortens the timeline

What speeds up a sale is what happens between the first message and the offer. Every Saturday spent showing the unit to someone who was never going to buy is a Saturday the real buyer didn't see it. If the listing already answered the basics, the first viewing can be the one that decides.

This is judgment from the trade, not a measurement. Panama has no public days-on-market statistic you could use to compare a complete listing against a weak one.

Every missing detail filters the wrong way

Serious buyers are short on time. They don't write to ask for the square meters; they move on to the next listing, which has them. The casual looker has time to spare, and writes. An incomplete listing ends up filling your phone with exactly the people least likely to buy.

Data from other markets points the same way. In the National Association of Realtors' buyer research, 89% of US buyers rated photos on a property site as very useful, and 86% said the same of detailed property information (NAR, 2021). That's the United States, not Panama.

What a listing should include, besides the photos:

  • Square meters, floor, parking spaces and the maintenance fee. In Panama, a condo building sits under a PH (propiedad horizontal, roughly a condominium regime: your unit, the common areas and the fee that keeps them up). What the fee covers varies by building, so ask the building administration.
  • Who the property makes sense for, and why, in a sentence or two. A description that just lists bedrooms means nothing to someone comparing five apartments.
  • The drawback, up front. West-facing, no pool in the building, the original kitchen. Whoever keeps writing after reading that has already priced it in, and the viewing is less likely to end in disappointment.
  • A price you can explain. It draws the people who do the math.

Outside the listing, have the paperwork ready before you publish: the finca (the property's record at Panama's Public Registry) and the building's paz y salvo (a certificate that the unit owes no fees). A serious buyer will ask for both, and if they're financing, so will their bank.

Photos get their own piece: a good property with bad photos looks like something else. Presenting well also isn't the same as renovating before you sell; most of the time it's enough to tidy up and describe the property fully.

When inquiries come in but go nowhere

If you're getting messages and nobody asks to visit, the presentation is attracting the wrong people, or the price is saying something the listing doesn't explain. If weeks go by and even a good listing brings no viewings, the price is the likeliest cause, and it's worth checking how it was set: a price without a method is just a rumor. The other reasons a property stalls are in if your property isn't selling.

If you've been on the market for months, before you touch the price, read your listing the way someone with four other tabs open would. Can they find the square meters, the fee and the drawback without having to write to you?

Frequently asked questions

Should I put the price in the listing?

In most cases, yes. In REA Group's Property Seeker survey (Australia, 2023), 72% of buyers said they skip listings that don't show a price. It's a different market, but the logic travels: the serious buyer moves on, and the one who does write asks "how much?" before anything else.

Does it help to list on several portals at once?

It helps people see it, as long as it's the same version everywhere: same photos, same price, same details. If the presentation is weak, more portals just repeat it.
