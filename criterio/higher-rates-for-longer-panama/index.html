Higher rates for longer: in Panama, that changes who has the negotiating advantage.

October 16, 2026

Expensive credit does not stop every transaction. It changes who can move, who has to wait and where the real negotiating room appears.

Interest rates are usually discussed as if they move the entire property market in the same direction.

They do not.

On July 29, 2026, the U.S. Federal Reserve kept its target range at 3.50% to 3.75%. Inflation was still above its 2% objective, and three members of the committee preferred to raise the range by another quarter point.

That does not tell us what every mortgage in Panama will cost next month. It does tell us that waiting for cheap money to return quickly is not a strategy.

Panama uses the U.S. dollar and has no domestic policy rate of its own. Changes in U.S. financial conditions tend to reach Panama through bank funding, liquidity and lending decisions, although the transmission is neither immediate nor identical across banks.

The practical effect is simple: expensive credit does not remove every buyer. It changes the balance between them.

The financed buyer feels the rate first

A buyer who needs a mortgage is not negotiating only with the seller. The bank is also defining the size of the decision.

The payment must fit. The appraisal must support the loan. The buyer must satisfy the bank's income, debt and equity requirements. When financing becomes more expensive, the same monthly budget supports a smaller loan.

That can lead to three different outcomes:

  • the buyer lowers the price range;
  • the buyer brings more equity;
  • or the buyer leaves the market for now.

This is where demand can thin before published prices visibly fall.

A property may remain listed at the same asking price while receiving fewer viable offers. The seller sees a price. The market sees fewer buyers who can actually close at that price.

For the financed buyer, the wrong response is to wait passively for rates to solve the problem. The useful response is to know the approved amount, the comfortable payment and the maximum cash contribution before negotiating.

Then the conversation is not «I hope the rate comes down.» It is «this is the price at which the transaction still works.»

Cash does not make every property a good deal

A cash buyer —or a buyer using limited financing— is less exposed to the monthly-payment constraint.

That does not mean the buyer should pay whatever is asked. It means the buyer may face less competition in the part of the market where financed demand has weakened.

The advantage is not simply having cash. It is being able to offer certainty:

  • fewer financing conditions;
  • a clearer closing timetable;
  • less risk that an appraisal breaks the transaction;
  • and greater capacity to act when the seller values execution over the headline price.

That certainty has value. It can create room to negotiate price, timing, included items, repairs or other terms.

But cash is not a reason to ignore the asset. A weak location, poor building management, an inefficient layout or an inflated asking price remains a weak decision even without a mortgage.

Panama is not one financing market

The effect is usually more visible in segments where the buyer depends on local financing: first homes, middle-market apartments and transactions where the down payment is already stretching the buyer's available cash.

In higher-priced segments —including conversations around areas such as Santa María or Punta Pacífica— buyers may arrive with larger equity positions, external income or cash. The rate can still affect the decision, but it may not be the variable that determines whether the buyer can transact.

This is a market observation, not a rule about every transaction in those areas.

The same distinction applies to sellers.

A seller with no urgent debt, no replacement purchase and no fixed deadline can wait longer. A seller carrying an expensive loan, an empty unit or another financial commitment may value certainty much more.

Two identical asking prices can therefore hide two very different negotiating positions.

That is why «the market is slow» is not enough. The useful questions are:

  • Which price band has lost financed buyers?
  • How long has the property been exposed?
  • Is the seller carrying a cost while waiting?
  • Does the buyer need an appraisal to support the agreed price?
  • Which term matters more to the seller: price, speed or certainty?

Do not negotiate the rate. Negotiate the transaction.

Neither the buyer nor the seller controls the next Federal Reserve decision.

They do control how a transaction is structured.

For a financed buyer, that means comparing the full cost of the loan, protecting the appraisal condition and avoiding a price that only works under an optimistic future rate.

For a cash buyer, it means using certainty as leverage without confusing leverage with value.

For a seller, it means distinguishing between an offer that looks higher and one that has a stronger probability of closing.

Rates matter. But they do not create one Panama market.

They expose where financing is essential, where cash is competing with fewer buyers and where a seller's real position is weaker than the listing suggests.

That is why I am not watching only the next Fed meeting. I am watching where the rate bites —and where it does not.
