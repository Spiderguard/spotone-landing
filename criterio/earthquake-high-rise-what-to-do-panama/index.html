After the earthquake, the question is for the building

During an earthquake on a high floor, official guidance says drop, cover and hold on, and skip the elevator; after it stops, your building's plan takes over.

Message me on WhatsApp

On October 9, just past midday, an earthquake centered in the Azuero region was felt strongly in Panama City. According to the PAHO/WHO situation report (in Spanish), the University of Panama's Geosciences Institute reported it at magnitude 7.5.

In many towers, the shaking felt like it went on for a long time. Doors banging, water sloshing out of pools, elevators stopped, people heading down the stairs while the building was still moving. And every building's WhatsApp group filled up with the same question: do I go down or stay put?

I'm not a structural engineer, and I won't give an opinion on whether any building is safe; that's an engineer's job. What I've done here is put the official guidance in order and, above all, the questions worth asking the building's administration, which is where an agent does have something to say.

What should you do in an earthquake if you live on a high floor in Panama?

During an earthquake on a high floor in Panama, official emergency guidance says to drop, cover your head and neck, and hold on until it stops, away from windows. Don't use the elevator or try to get out while it's moving. Once it stops, follow the instructions of your building's administration and the authorities.

General guidance and your building's protocol

The general advice comes from civil protection. In Panama, SINAPROC, the national civil protection agency, says not to use elevators, to stay away from glass windows, and not to go back into a building until it has been checked (its guidance on earthquakes, published May 5, 2023). Guides like Ready.gov, from the US emergency management agency, and the Earthquake Country Alliance say the same. It applies to almost any modern building.

Your building's protocol is set by the administration of the PH (propiedad horizontal, Panama's condominium regime): evacuation routes, the meeting point, who inspects the elevators and when people can go back up. If it exists and it's well done, it rules. If there's no written protocol, or nobody knows where it is, that's your first finding.

What should you do while it's shaking?

Ready.gov and the Earthquake Country Alliance recommend getting down on your hands and knees, covering your head and neck, ideally under a sturdy table, and holding on until it stops. If there's no table nearby, get next to an interior wall, away from windows. For tall buildings, the Earthquake Country Alliance adds: don't use the elevator, and don't try to get out while it's moving. If you're in bed, Ready.gov says stay there and protect your head with a pillow.

ShakeOut explains why: studies of earthquake injuries and deaths in the U.S. show you're much more likely to be hurt by objects that fall or get thrown around than to die in a collapsed building. That's why it calls standing in a doorway outdated advice, and the "triangle of life" discredited.

The Earthquake Country Alliance has specific instructions for people who use a wheelchair, walker or cane. If someone like that lives with you, read them beforehand, not during.

Once it stops

The guides say to expect aftershocks: if one comes, protect yourself where you are. Check for injuries, the smell of gas, running water or sparks, and put shoes on before walking around, since there may be broken glass.

If you see obvious damage, like new cracks in columns or beams, doors that no longer close, or the smell of gas or smoke, the guides say to get out and not go back in until it's been inspected. Once outside, keep away from the facade. If there's no visible damage, follow the instructions of the administration and SINAPROC.

The elevator is off the table until the administration confirms it's been checked. Many modern systems stop the car at the nearest floor when they detect shaking and stay out of service until a technician resets them.

If the tower is on the waterfront, tsunami instructions come from the authorities, not the WhatsApp group.

In the days after, in your unit

A hairline crack in the plaster isn't the same as a crack in a column, beam or slab, or a door that's been knocked out of square. Whatever you can't read, report to the administration in writing and have someone qualified look at it. The guides also insist on anchoring water heaters, tall furniture, TVs and mirrors to the wall: it costs little and prevents a lot.

If you rent, tell the owner about any damage in writing, with photos and a date. If you're the owner, document it and reply the same way. If you're thinking about ending your lease after the earthquake, I covered that separately.

What should you ask your building's administration?

An earthquake plan gets written beforehand, by the building. Don't raise it as a complaint. These are questions any board should be able to answer:

  • Is there a written, up-to-date emergency plan? Where is it posted?
  • Where is the meeting point, and is it away from the facade?
  • Who inspects the elevators after an earthquake, and who authorizes using them again?
  • Was the building inspected after October 9? Who did it, and can owners see the report?
  • Are the stairwells clear and lit, with working emergency lights?
  • Is there a list of residents with limited mobility, so people know who to help first?
  • Does the building have seismic instrumentation? If so, who reviews the readings?
  • When was the last drill?

Frequently asked questions

Is it safer to live on a low floor?

The official guidance reviewed here doesn't say a low floor is safer in an earthquake. What changes is logistics: it's easier to get down if you need to, and to live with the elevator out of service.

Are Panama's buildings prepared?

There's no single answer, because they weren't all designed under the same code. What you can find out is which code your building was designed under and what inspections it has had. Whether a particular tower is safe is for a structural engineer to say, not an agent or an article.
