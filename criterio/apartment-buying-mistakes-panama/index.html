The list that protects you is the one that rules things out

The most common apartment-buying mistakes in Panama can be avoided before the viewing: commute, noise, paperwork, water and power backup, condo fee and resale.

Message me on WhatsApp

Most people start an apartment search with a list of what they want: a pool, a gym, a balcony, an ocean view. That list fills itself, and every viewing adds something to it.

Very few write the other list, the one with whatever rules a property out before you see it. That's the list that looks after you.

What are the most common mistakes when buying an apartment in Panama?

The most common mistakes when buying an apartment in Panama can be avoided before a viewing: a commute you can't live with, noise you can't fix, papers that don't line up, a building with no water or power backup, a condo fee that doesn't match what you get, and a unit that's hard to resell. Filter first; amenities later.

Think in reverse

Instead of looking for the apartment you like most, you look for the one you'd end up regretting, and you learn to spot it from the listing. It's an old way of thinking through almost any problem: ask what would guarantee failure, then work to avoid it.

You'll use the building gym a few times a week, if that. A bad commute you pay for twice a day, every day you live there.

What rules out an apartment before you see it?

When you have several options in front of you, start by crossing off, not by ranking. These are the filters that cross off the most.

The real commute. Time it at the hour you'd actually leave, which is almost never the hour of the viewing. Some apartments fail on that alone, and living farther out tends to cost more than it looks.

Noise that isn't up to you. A main avenue, a nightclub, an approved construction project next door. Go back at least once after dark.

The papers. The finca (the property as it's registered at Panama's Public Registry) should be in the seller's name, and any lien, such as a mortgage or another recorded charge, should have an explanation. Ask for the finca number and the monthly condo fee by message before you set up a visit. The paz y salvo (a certificate that the unit is current on its fees to the PH, the condo association) comes later, once there's an offer, because the notary requires it for the deed. If the paperwork drags with no explanation, make a note of it.

Water and power when they fail. Ask whether the building has a reserve water tank, what the backup generator covers (the elevators? your air conditioning?) and whether the street floods in a heavy downpour.

The fee against what you get. A high condo fee with elevators that break down and neglected common areas is worse than a high fee you can see in every hallway. Ask what it covers and compare that with what you see when you walk in. The opposite case, a low maintenance fee that isn't always good news, comes with its own questions.

The unit nobody else would want. An awkward layout, a low floor looking at a wall, a single parking space in a building full of families. It may not bother you today; it will the day you sell. A good location doesn't guarantee your unit rises with it, and size matters at resale too.

What's left after you cross things off?

What's left isn't always the prettiest option, but it doesn't come with expensive mistakes. That's when the view, the finishes and the pool come in, and that's when it's time to sit down with the three numbers worth checking before you make an offer and look at the property as a whole.

You go to fewer viewings, and the ones you go to count for more. You end up seeing three properties that already passed the test instead of ten that will only confuse you.

Frequently asked questions

What if nothing on my list passes?

It happens. When it does, the filter is usually telling you something about the budget or the area more than about the properties. Before loosening it, decide which filters you can live without and which you can't, and only move on the ones you can.
