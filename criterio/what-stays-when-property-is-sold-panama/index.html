The property sold. Did the chandelier?

When a property is sold in Panama, what stays for certain is what the offer and contract name as included; showing up in photos or being installed isn't enough.

A buyer tours an apartment with light fixtures, curtains, air conditioners, appliances and some furniture, and makes an offer assuming all of it comes with the sale.

Say the owner accepts the price and, a few days before signing, takes down the dining-room chandelier because it's a family piece.

Both sides thought they had agreed to the same thing. The chandelier was never on paper.

What stays in a house when it's sold in Panama?

In a Panama property sale, what stays for certain is what the contract names as included: light fixtures, air conditioners, curtains, appliances or furniture. Items fixed to the property may count as part of it, but each one can be argued. Listing photos don't settle it. The list of inclusions and exclusions should start with the offer.

What exactly is being sold?

What passes from one owner to the next is the finca: the property as it is recorded at Panama's Public Registry. Everything else, from the chandelier to the air conditioners, is worth naming.

You'll hear two rules of thumb: "anything attached stays" and "furniture never goes." Neither one protects you. Panama's Civil Code (Article 325) treats as part of the property whatever is fixed to it in a way that can't be removed without breaking something, along with certain items the owner placed there with the intent of leaving them permanently. Whether a particular light or AC unit falls into that is a question for lawyers, and it depends on how it's installed and what the paperwork says. The practical answer is simpler: if it matters, name it.

Where the disputes show up

Almost always in the same places. Decorative lights and ceiling fans. Air conditioners and their remotes. Curtains, blinds and rods. Appliances: what Panamanians call línea blanca means fridge and stove to one person and includes the washer and dryer to another. Rentals have the same problem, and what listings actually declare under that term is in what "línea blanca" includes. Built-in cabinetry versus loose furniture. Water heaters, pumps and filters. Solar panels and their batteries, or an EV charger. And the small things nobody writes down: remotes, keys, manuals, spare parts.

An item showing up in the listing photos doesn't prove it's included. Neither does the fact that it's plugged in.

When should the list of what stays be drawn up?

The first version of the list travels with the offer. It doesn't appear three days before the deed. For anything that really matters, the list says whether it's included or excluded, a description no one can misread (brand or model where it applies), how many there are and where, what condition they're handed over in, whether they work, and what happens if one breaks before closing.

"Appliances included" is still ambiguous. "The fridge, stove and washer currently in the apartment" isn't.

From there, the list moves into the purchase agreement (promesa de compraventa, the contract that sets price, payment terms and dates before the deed), and your lawyer reviews it before you sign. If the owner wants to keep a piece, exclude it from the start. If the buyer cares about a piece of equipment, put it in the offer instead of taking it for granted.

The final walk-through

Before signing, it's worth doing one last walk-through with the list in hand. That's when you check that the included items are still there, that the excluded ones were removed without damage, that remotes and keys are accounted for, that the equipment works as agreed and that any change was accepted in writing. Also that nothing was left behind that the buyer didn't ask for: an old sofa or a room full of boxes is a problem on handover day too.

A chandelier a week before closing

A sale worth hundreds of thousands of dollars can stall over a light fixture. The fixture is worth little; what breaks is trust between the two sides, right when they're closest to done. So the rule is short: if an item weighed on the decision, it goes in the offer in writing, even if it seems obvious.

Frequently asked questions

What if the seller swaps an appliance for a cheaper one before handover?

If the list names the brand or model, the swap is obvious and can be raised. If it only says "air conditioner," it's hard to push back. Any substitution both sides accept should be in writing before closing.
