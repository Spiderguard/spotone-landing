The property that survives a bad year

After buying a home in Panama, keep enough savings to get through a bad year without selling: months of mortgage, condo fees and fixed costs, plus one major repair.

Message me on WhatsApp

A drop that sounds small in a headline can wipe out half of what you have in an apartment.

A hypothetical example: you buy at $250,000 with 20% down. You put in $50,000 and the bank lends you $200,000. If the price falls 10%, the apartment is worth $225,000, and you still owe the bank almost the same amount. Your share went from $50,000 to $25,000.

In Panama, that drop doesn't always show up in the asking price. It can also show up as months without a buyer, which is the same loss wearing a different face.

How much savings do you need after buying a home in Panama?

The savings you need after buying a home in Panama are whatever gets you through a bad year without selling: several months of mortgage payments, condo fees and fixed costs, plus a cushion for one major repair. Keep it in a separate account. The amount depends on your income and your debt.

What does a bad year look like?

It rarely comes one thing at a time. The tenant gives notice and the apartment sits empty for a few months. At the same time, a leak means opening up a wall.

Then there's the special assessment. It gets approved at a meeting of the PH (the condo owners' association, under Panama's horizontal property regime) that you didn't attend, to waterproof a roof that isn't yours, and you pay your share anyway.

There's one people forget. If your mortgage rate isn't fixed, it can change over the life of the loan. Before you sign, ask whether it's fixed, variable or adjustable, what benchmark it's tied to and how often it can move. A rate going up is a bad year too.

And the simplest one, which is the hardest: income that stops for a few months. Over the years a mortgage runs, expect at least one of these.

Buying at your limit

A purchase made at your limit can go fine for five straight years and fall apart in one. In that year the property is usually fine. The one who isn't is the owner who bought with no margin and now has to sell when they don't want to, at whatever price they're offered.

The bank tells you how much it can lend you on today's income. It doesn't tell you how much you should owe if that income changes, and only you can answer that second question. Buying at exactly the approved amount leaves no room to breathe: the monthly payment fits the budget, the repair doesn't. Before you decide how much to put down, or whether you're better off paying cash or financing, set aside the money that won't be touched.

Should the reserve be separate from the down payment?

The down payment isn't your savings. If you emptied every account to reach the down payment, you bought with no margin.

Near closing, it's easy to arrive with just enough for the down payment and closing costs, and plan to furnish the place with whatever is left. The first surprise ends up on a credit card, which is usually the most expensive debt in the house.

Two rules help. First, a separate account with its own name, the property's, that isn't touched for furniture or trips, or for extra mortgage payments until it's full; before you prepay, also ask whether your loan charges anything for paying early. Second, if you're going to buy to rent, the reserve should also cover empty months, because the rent in the lease isn't what you collect every month of the year.

Before you sign, write down how you'd pay for a year in which all of the above happens together. If the honest answer is "by selling," the purchase isn't ready yet: a purchase is tested against the bad year, not the average one.

Frequently asked questions

What if I've already bought at my limit?

You don't need to sell tomorrow. Start building the reserve, even slowly, look at which fixed costs you can cut and don't take on new debt until you have a cushion. If your income stops, talk to the bank before you fall behind, not after.
