Which structural code was your building designed under?

October 16, 2026

A building from 1998 and one approved under REP‑2021 did not begin with the same framework. The date helps, but it cannot answer the question alone.

After an earthquake, a question rarely found in a property listing becomes relevant: which structural code was this building designed under?

The answer cannot declare a tower safe or unsafe. It can establish that Panama City's buildings were not all calculated under the same regulations, technical references or seismic information.

That is more useful than offering blanket reassurance that nobody can support.

The code has changed

Panama has updated its Structural Design Regulation, known as the REP, several times.

The relevant sequence is:

YearFrameworkUseful reading
1976Panama Seismic CodePreceded the first REP
1984First REPEstablished a domestic structural regulation
1994REP‑94Introduced a seismic-instrumentation provision
1998Instrumentation standardsDeveloped requirements for certain buildings
2004REP‑2004Updated the structural regulation
2014REP‑2014Formally adopted in 2015
2021REP‑2021Adopted in 2022; effective from 30 April 2023

REP‑2021 is the current framework. It was adopted through Resolution JTIA‑020‑2022. Its Annex 1 concerning performance-based seismic design was subsequently modified, so technical claims about its application should refer to the applicable version and resolution.

Construction year is not enough

Saying that a building dates from 2018 does not by itself prove which code governed every part of its design.

Distinguish among the structural design date, plan approval, construction permit, start of work, occupancy permit and later structural interventions.

A project may have been designed under one code, processed during a transition and completed years later. The commercial age of the building is evidence to investigate, not a technical opinion. It is the same distinction that appears when comparing new or resale.

What seismic instrumentation means

Instrumentation records how a structure moves during certain events. In Panama, the provision is documented from REP‑94 and was developed through specific standards in 1998 for certain buildings, including thresholds related to height and constructed area.

An accelerograph is not a safety certificate.

It produces data. Its usefulness depends on installation, operation, maintenance, access to records, professional analysis and follow-up. Without evidence, one should not assume that every building subject to the requirement is instrumented or that enforcement is uniform.

What a buyer or owner can ask

Without attempting to replace a structural engineer, a buyer can request information:

  • When were the structural plans approved?
  • Which REP version appears in the project file or calculations?
  • Are structural plans and the geotechnical study available?
  • Does the building have seismic instrumentation, is it operational and who keeps the records?
  • Were technical inspections performed after relevant events?
  • Are there repairs, reports or meeting minutes related to structural or non-structural damage?
  • Which later interventions affected building elements?

The absence of an immediate answer does not prove a defect. It identifies an unresolved part of due diligence. Minutes and the reserve fund say more than they appear to, as seen in the maintenance fee.

What a code cannot guarantee

A code establishes design criteria. By itself, it does not guarantee that construction matched the design precisely, that materials and controls met every specification, that later alterations did not occur, that maintenance was adequate or that a specific building will avoid damage in a future event.

Not every visible crack is structural damage either. Finishes and other non-structural components may crack or detach. A competent inspection is needed to determine what happened.

The question «what year is it from?» should begin the conversation, not end it. And if the doubt arose during a lease, keep it separate from ending the contract.

The date places the building in a generation. Documents and technical assessment say much more.
