"A neighbor sold for X" isn't data yet

To tell if a price is fair in Panama, rank your references by what you can prove: verifiable sales, secondhand sale prices, the appraisal and, last of all, listings.

Where does your price come from? If a buyer asks you during a showing, what do you show them?

There's almost always an answer. "A neighbor sold for X." "The appraisal says X." "There's an identical one listed online for X." All three sound like data. None of them, on its own, tells you how well the number holds up in front of someone about to put down real money.

How do you tell if an apartment price is fair in Panama?

To tell if an apartment price is fair in Panama, rank your references by what you can prove about each one: first, verifiable sales in the same building or floor plan; then sale prices passed on by someone who was in the deal; then the appraisal and its comparables; last, listings. A price with backing comes from cross-checking them.

Why isn't a single data point enough in Panama?

In Panama, the sale price goes into the deed, but there is no public database of sales you can search easily. What you see, almost always, are asking prices. Everyone works with loose pieces, and the gap between a defensible price and an inflated one comes down to how you weigh them.

From firmest to weakest, the order is this:

  • A verifiable sale in the same building or the same floor plan. A recent sale you can back up with the deed or with someone who signed it. If it's also a unit with the same square meters and a similar view, it outweighs everything else combined.
  • A secondhand sale price. Someone tells you what a unit sold for. It's worth as much as the person telling you, and whether they were part of the deal.
  • The appraisal. A number built with a method, but for a specific purpose. The useful part is its comparables and its breakdown of areas, more than the figure on the cover.
  • The asking price. What others are asking. It tells you what you'll be compared against, not what price has backing.
  • What "people say." It carries no weight, but plenty of negotiations start there.

What should you ask about a secondhand sale price?

A sale price someone passes on to you is the most common reference and the easiest to inflate without meaning to. When someone gives you one, there are four things to ask. First, which exact unit: floor, line, square meters, because "one in the tower" could be the penthouse. Then, when: a sale from several years ago says little about today's price. Then, on what terms: cash or financed, furnished or empty, in a rush or not; a sale between relatives or out of an estate isn't a market reference. And who knows it firsthand, the owner who sold or the agent who closed it. If it comes thirdhand, it drops a step.

One that passes all four moves up almost to the first level. One that passes none counts as what "people say."

The appraisal and the listing

As a reference, the best part of an appraisal is in the pages nobody reads. Ask for the full report and look at which units it was compared against and with what adjustments. The cover figure was made for another purpose, which is why the appraisal isn't the price. If the buyer is financing, the bank will also order its own, and that's where your reference gets tested: a property is worth what someone pays, until the bank measures it.

The listing is the most visible reference and the weakest. An asking price can change next week, and what's asked isn't what was paid. A listing that has been up for a long time tells you, at most, the price at which the market didn't buy.

How the number comes together

With one firm reference, the range can be narrow. With only listings, it's wide, and you have to say so. A wide range that holds is worth more than a narrow one that falls apart at the first question.

On their own, buyers rarely reach the first two steps; to move up, you need someone who has been part of deals in that building. If there's no sale in the same building, look for the closest match in the area, similar in age and amenities, and adjust for the differences.

A price reference weighs what you can prove about it, and the buyer across from you will run the same test, with you or without you.

Frequently asked questions

Is the average price per square meter in the area a useful reference?

It helps you get your bearings, not set a price. An area average mixes buildings of different ages and conditions, and it can't see the view, the floor or the state of the unit. Use it to spot a number that's way off, then narrow it down to the building.
