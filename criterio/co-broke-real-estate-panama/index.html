Two agents, one point of contact

Sharing a client with another agent works when the ground rules are set before anyone opens the apartment door, not after.

Message me on WhatsApp

It happens all the time. A client messages two agents on the same day, both send the same apartment, and both end up, by different routes, at the same door.

Nobody did anything wrong. The client wanted options and the agents did their job. What's usually missing is an agreement before the showing.

What is a co-broke in real estate?

A co-broke is when two real estate agents share a single deal: usually one works with the buyer or tenant and the other has the property, and they settle between themselves what each one gets. It works when, before the first showing, both are clear on who introduced the client, who talks to whom and where information flows.

Sharing deals with other agents is simply how I work. For the client, the essentials don't change: there is still one person who answers.

Two situations that look alike and aren't

The first is the orderly one: one agent has the client, another has the property, and they agree before anything moves.

The second is where friction starts: two agents show up with the same client at the same property. The custom among agents is to ask who introduced the client first and whether they can show it, not who has the stronger claim. It's a professional custom, which is why whatever was put in writing carries so much weight.

Most of that friction is avoided with one question before sending a listing: "Have you already seen it, or did someone else send it to you?"

What gets agreed before the showing

  • That the other agent is licensed. In Panama, working as a real estate broker requires a license from the Real Estate Technical Board, the Junta Técnica de Bienes Raíces (Decree-Law 6 of 1999, art. 2). Asking for the license number before sharing a deal doesn't offend anyone who has one.
  • The client registration, in writing. It's the notice to the other agent that this client is yours. One message is enough: client's name, property, date and who is introducing them. It goes out before the address does.
  • Who talks to whom. The client talks to their agent; the owner, to theirs. If the owner and the client start negotiating directly during the showing, both agents should know, so nobody keeps working with a different price.
  • Who sets up the showing. One person proposes times and confirms. Two people arranging the same appointment is the fastest way for the client to arrive and find nobody with the key.
  • Where information goes. Photos, floor plans, the finca number (the property's record at Panama's Public Registry), the building's maintenance fee and any offer all go through the same thread between the two agents. That way nobody is working from an old version of the price.

It takes a couple of messages, and it saves you from piecing the story back together just as an offer is on its way.

If you're the client

You don't need to know how agents sort things out between themselves. What you should notice is the result: one person answers you, confirms the showing and sends you the paperwork. You don't get three versions of the same apartment or two calls for the same appointment.

It's reasonable to expect your agent to tell you from the start if another agent is working on the owner's side. And one simple thing helps from your end: if you've contacted several agents, tell each of them which properties you've already seen and who sent them.

The deal also moves faster. When the owner knows who they're dealing with and the client knows who to ask, the offer comes in sooner and the promesa de compraventa (the preliminary purchase agreement that sets price, deadlines and conditions) gets negotiated without crossed wires.

The same tangle, seen from the buyer who calls everyone, is in working with many agents; from the owner's side, in one agent representing it, or five just listing it.

When the client came through someone else

Sometimes there's no registration, or there are two. If another agent introduced the client first, the decent thing is to say so and give them their due. If it isn't clear, the two agents talk it through before the client has to hear about it.

Arguing over a client at the apartment door costs the client the showing and costs both agents that owner's trust. And in a market the size of Panama's, the same agents tend to run into each other again on the next deal, with that owner or another.
