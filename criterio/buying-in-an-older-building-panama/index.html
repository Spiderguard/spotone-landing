The 30-year-old building that's still full

An older building in Panama is worth buying when it can prove on paper that it was looked after: recent minutes, a funded contingency fund and fees that get collected.

Message me on WhatsApp

A thirty-year-old building scares off a lot of buyers. It doesn't scare the people who have lived there for twenty.

That gap tells you more than the construction date. If a building with three decades behind it is still full, with people who stay and units that rarely show up on the portals, it has done something right that no new project can prove yet. Time is the one report nobody can dress up.

Is it worth buying in an older building in Panama?

It is when the building can show, on paper, that it has been looked after: recent association meeting minutes, the contingency fund the law requires (the Fondo para Imprevistos) with money in it, major repairs identified and budgeted and fees that actually get collected. Age alone says little. How it aged and how it's run say much more.

Buying new or resale is a choice about what you value. This piece is about something else: what time tells you about one specific building.

Thirty years leave a paper trail

There's an old rule about things that last: whatever has worked for decades has a good chance of continuing to work, because it already survived what took others down. For a building, it only counts if it kept working the whole time. Still standing isn't enough.

In Panama, condo buildings are run under the propiedad horizontal regime, usually just called the PH: shared common areas and a fee every owner pays. A PH that has run that way for decades has been through several boards, changes of administrator, owners who stopped paying and at least one repair nobody wanted to approve. All of that left a record.

A project sold off plans has no record yet. And what a rendering can't tell you is exactly what's most expensive to find out later: whether owners pay, whether the assembly approves what needs approving or kicks it to next year, how the decision will get made the day the elevators have to be replaced.

A cheap test: ask around the building, without asking for names, whether any of the original owners are still there. If the answer comes without hesitation, the building has something that keeps people. If nobody knows, because the units change tenants every year, put that in the other column.

What paperwork should you ask for before making an offer?

  • The minutes of the last few assemblies, in full. They show what gets discussed, what gets approved and, above all, what gets postponed. An issue that shows up in several sets of minutes in a row without being resolved is already a pending repair.
  • The state of the contingency fund: whether there's money in it and whether it's been used for routine expenses, which Law 284 of 2022 doesn't allow. That fund is for the unforeseen; big replacements, like the elevators, are paid from savings or a special assessment the owners' assembly approves.
  • Major repairs and special assessments, approved or under discussion. If one was approved before you buy, put in writing in the purchase agreement who pays whatever is still outstanding.
  • Arrears, as a question: "What share of owners are behind on their fees?" You don't need the exact number; the point is whether the administration knows it and tells you without getting uncomfortable. It may also show up in the reports presented to the assembly.
  • The unit's paz y salvo (the certificate showing it owes no fees to the PH). Unpaid fees stay attached to the unit, whoever owns it, and the notary requires the certificate for the transfer.

And one question for the bank, before you make an offer: whether the building's age or the appraisal changes the term or the amount it will lend you.

A low fee in an older building deserves a second look, because a low maintenance fee isn't always good news. If the building is several decades old, also ask which structural code it was designed under.

When does time work against the building?

Some thirty-year-old buildings have reinvested almost nothing: original plumbing, untreated facade, a contingency fund that went to paying the electric bill. That building hasn't proven anything. It's behind, and the next owner inherits the bill.

Before you fall for the view, ask for the minutes and read them yourself. Read some from a few years back too: that's where you see what was promised and whether it got fixed. Keep in mind that Panama's current condo law dates from 2022, so minutes from ten years ago were written under different rules.

Frequently asked questions

Who gives me the minutes and the state of the contingency fund?

You ask the seller. As the owner, they're entitled to get them from the building's administration. For the paz y salvo, the law sets a maximum of three calendar days, and the administrator also has to give a copy of the minutes to any owner who requests it. The fund shows up in the administrator's reports and the budget. If weeks go by, ask why. Which documents you can demand, and when, is something to go over with your lawyer before you sign the purchase agreement (promesa de compraventa, the contract that comes before the deed).

Is an older building harder to resell?

It depends less on age than on how the building looks and how it's run. A well-kept one competes. A neglected one gets marked down at every showing, because the buyer walks through the lobby before seeing the unit.
