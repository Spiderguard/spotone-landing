Would your agent buy it?

Almost everyone who advises you on a purchase loses nothing if you get it wrong. One question puts them in your shoes, and you can ask it without offending anyone.

Message me on WhatsApp

"Would you buy it?"

You can ask any agent that, and very few clients do. It feels awkward, as if you doubted them.

How do you know if a real estate agent in Panama is trustworthy?

To know whether a real estate agent in Panama is trustworthy, first check their license in the official registry of licensed brokers, and make sure it is in their name, not just the company's. Then ask whether they would buy the property they are recommending, in your situation. A good agent answers plainly. A dodge is an answer too.

Check the license before the first viewing

Anyone working as a real estate broker in Panama needs a license from the Junta Técnica de Bienes Raíces (the Real Estate Technical Board), part of the Ministry of Commerce and Industries (MICI). The board runs a public lookup of licensed brokers. Ask for the number and their full name, and look them up. The board has asked that the number appear in listings, so you can often check it before you even reach out.

Make sure the license is in the agent's own name. The board has been clear that every agent needs an individual license: the company holding one does not cover the person showing you around. It works the same if you are still abroad. Anyone who has a license will give you the number without fuss. Mine is PN-1240.

A license tells you someone is allowed to practice. It doesn't tell you whether their advice fits you.

Most advice comes from people who don't pay for the mistake

When you buy, the people advising you lose nothing if it goes wrong. The friend who swore that neighborhood was about to take off still lives where he always did. Last night's video won't make your mortgage payment. Advice from someone who doesn't carry the consequences weighs less than advice from someone who does, even when both know the same things.

That includes me. Any agent is better off if the purchase goes through, and if it goes badly, the agent isn't the one living next to the construction site or trying to sell into a slow market. So an opinion isn't enough. The agent has to stand in your place and tell you what they would do with their own money.

How to ask without sounding like an accusation

"Are you telling me the truth?" puts anyone on the defensive. These work better:

  • "If it were your money and your situation, would you buy it?"
  • "What do you like least about this one?"

The second one gets you further. Every property has something: the monthly building fee, the commute, the afternoon sun hitting the living room, a tandem parking space (one car behind the other, so you coordinate with another driver every morning). If a whole conversation goes by and nothing negative comes up, what you heard was the listing read out loud.

If you're buying to rent out, adjust the first one: "Would you buy it as a rental? Who would rent it?"

And if the agent is the one representing the owner, don't expect "I wouldn't buy it." Ask what they like least. That they can answer: what anyone can see on a viewing isn't the owner's secret.

What a good agent says

There is more than one honest answer, and none of them is "it's a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity":

  • "Yes, for you it makes sense," with reasons tied to your life, not to the property in the abstract.
  • "Not for your situation, though for another buyer it works."
  • "I don't know yet. First I want to see the building's meeting minutes and the paz y salvo (the certificate showing the unit is current on its building fees)."
  • "I wouldn't buy it, and here's why."

That last one should earn the most trust. Saying "I wouldn't buy it" can cost them a viewing, sometimes a sale, which is why it's worth listening when they say it.

The signs that call for a second opinion are different. Answers about the market in general ("Panama always goes up") instead of this unit. Pressure to grab a bargain that'll be "gone today," which tends to look more like an Instagram bargain than a real one. A monthly payment you're told you'll "figure out." Or a price that arrives with no explanation, which is a price without a method.

None of these makes someone a bad professional. It happens to anyone in this business: getting more excited about the property than about the person who will live in it. If it keeps happening, get another opinion before you sign.

Frequently asked questions

Does the agent showing me the property work for me or for the owner?

It depends. Often there are two: one who has the listing with the owner and one who works with you, and they coordinate with each other (in Panama this is called co-broke). Ask each of them, in the first message, who they work with. If you buy through a platform, the question is the same: who represents you.
