One rent, three numbers

A rent roll lists a building's units, tenants and rents in Panama, and you read it by checking contract rent against collected rent and market rent.

Message me on WhatsApp

Say a building has eight apartments, each rented at $1,000 a month. The rent roll says $8,000 a month.

Last month, $5,850 came in.

Both numbers can be true at the same time, and the gap between them holds much of what you need to know before you make an offer.

What is a rent roll and how do you read it?

A rent roll is the list of a building's units with the tenant, the monthly rent agreed in the lease and the dates of each contract. In Panama it's often called the cuadro de alquileres. You read it by comparing three numbers per unit: what the lease says, what reached the account and what the market would pay today.

What three numbers does each unit have?

Contract rent is what the lease says. It's what the rent roll usually shows.

Collected rent is what reached the bank account. That's where you find arrears, discounts agreed on a handshake and empty units the rent roll still lists at their last price.

Market rent is what someone would pay today for that same unit if it were rented out again.

In the example, the gap between $8,000 and $5,850 breaks down like this:

  • Five apartments pay in full: $5,000.
  • One pays $850, because the owner gave the tenant a discount a while back and never put it in the lease.
  • One is two months behind.
  • One is empty, but the rent roll lists it at $1,000.

And if an identical apartment in that building rents for $1,100 today, the market would say $8,800.

Three numbers for the same rent: $8,000, $5,850 and $8,800. None of them is a lie. Each one answers a different question.

The columns worth asking for

If the rent roll arrives as a list of units and prices, it's worth asking for it to be filled in with:

  • Unit and size. To compare rent per square meter between units in the same building.
  • Tenant, identified by a code, not a name. What matters is whether it's a person or a company, and how long they've been there.
  • Lease start and end dates. If half the leases expire in the same quarter, half the income gets renegotiated at once.
  • Contract rent and collected rent, in two separate columns.
  • Security deposit (the amount each lease sets), and where that money is today.
  • Arrears, in months and in dollars, with the date of the last payment received.

Always ask for that last date. A tenant who owes a month and paid last week is not the same as one who owes a month and hasn't paid in three.

How do you check a rent roll?

The seller puts the rent roll together. To cross-check it, ask for the last twelve months of statements from the account the rent goes into, or the receipts if rent is collected some other way. If the collected figures on the rent roll don't match what was deposited, that's where the conversation starts.

Ask for the leases too. Sometimes the rent roll shows one rent and the lease another, and that gap gets cleared up before you make an offer.

What the third number tells you

If market rent is above contract rent, there may be room at renewal. How much, and when, depends on each lease and on Panama's rental rules, so ask before you offer and don't count it as income. If market rent is below, the risk runs the other way: at renewal, income can go down.

The rent roll is the starting point. Next comes subtracting expenses and empty months, which gets you to net income, and that math is in buy to rent. If you're torn between a building that produces income and one that's a bet on appreciation, the question changes: some real estate rises, other real estate works. And if you're on the other side, with a building to sell, this same rent roll, complete and backed by bank statements, is the first thing worth having ready.

Before you make an offer, ask for the other two numbers. Rent comes in three, and the rent roll usually shows only the one in the lease.

Frequently asked questions

What if the seller doesn't have a rent roll?

Ask for the leases and the last twelve months of bank statements, and build it yourself with the columns in this piece. If there are no signed leases either, that's where the conversation starts.

Does the same apply to a single rented apartment?

Yes, as a one-row rent roll. Before you buy an apartment with a tenant in place, ask for the lease and the past year of payments, and compare what was agreed with what actually came in.
