The bank lends on what you can prove

Before it lends you anything, the bank looks at what your paperwork shows: income, debts, credit history, down payment. Better to look at it yourself first.

Message me on WhatsApp

How much do you earn, according to your paperwork?

It may not be the number in your head. It's an easy question to skip while you're walking through an apartment with great light. The bank starts there.

What are the mortgage requirements in Panama?

Mortgage requirements in Panama center on your ability to pay: your income and how stable it is, an employment letter or tax returns if you're self-employed, your credit history with the APC, your current debts, the down payment and where it comes from, the property's appraisal, and the insurance attached to the loan.

These are the documents banks typically ask for. Each bank has its own exact list:

  • Panamanian ID (cédula). If you're a foreigner: passport, immigration ID card (carné migratorio) and work permit.
  • If you're salaried: an employment letter, pay stubs and Social Security records.
  • If you're self-employed: tax returns and a tax clearance certificate from the DGI, Panama's tax authority.
  • Bank statements, especially for the account that holds the down payment.
  • The purchase agreement (promesa de compraventa), once you have a property.

If you're not from here

A Panamanian bank can lend to a foreigner, but the file looks different. Expect the immigration documents above, and expect that the down payment and the term may not match what a resident gets. Ask for those conditions in writing before you commit to a property.

If you've never borrowed in Panama, the APC may have little or nothing on you, so ask the bank what it will accept instead.

What you earn, and for how long

If you're an employee, the bank cares about how much you earn and how long you've been earning it. A new job can count against you even if it pays more, so if you're about to switch, ask first. Age matters too: the later you borrow, the fewer years may be left to pay.

If you're self-employed, this is where many people get a surprise: the bank lends on what your returns and statements can prove, not on what you bill. It may measure your payment against your net taxable income, what you declare after deductions. Ask how many years of returns it will review, because one weak year can work against you, and talk to your accountant well before you apply.

Your debts and the APC

The APC is the credit bureau that holds your credit history in Panama, and the bank can only check it with your written authorization. Pull your own report early: under article 28 of Law 24 of 2002, as amended by Law 14 of 2006, if you show up in person with your ID, the bureau must tell you your credit history for free and give you a simple, unofficial report at no cost; a formal certification may carry a fee. An old card with a balance or someone else's debt under your name can take more than a week to fix, and you'd rather find it before the bank does.

Your debts count as much as your income. A car payment, credit cards and a personal loan all shrink what you can borrow for the home. If you're about to finance a car, talk to the bank first; doing it the other way around can cost you the approval.

The down payment, and where it comes from

The bank will want to see the down payment and know where it came from; checking the source of funds is part of its due diligence. Savings that have sat in your account for a while explain themselves. A large last-minute deposit, money a relative lends or gives you, or funds wired from abroad need documentation: ask which documents before you move the money. If you haven't settled on an amount yet, start with how much to put down.

The property gets examined too

The bank orders an appraisal, an appraiser's estimate of value for the bank, which isn't the same as the price. It decides how much to lend based on that result, your ability to pay and its own policies.

The loan's insurance is normally included in the monthly payment. Ask for the full payment, insurance and all, not the online calculator figure, and ask for the amortization table: principal, interest, the total, and whether the rate is fixed or adjustable. With rates staying higher for longer, the same payment buys a smaller loan.

You may read about Panama's preferential mortgage interest, a government rate subsidy. It changed in January 2026, under Law 468 of 2025 as amended by Law 481, and is now limited to lower-priced homes. If your purchase is near that range, ask your bank whether it qualifies and up to what price.

Get prequalified before you go looking

Prequalification is the bank's review of your numbers before you have a property, to tell you roughly how much it could lend. It isn't final approval, but it changes two things.

First, you search within a real range. Touring places you can't afford has a cost: afterwards, what you can afford may look small.

Second, it changes the negotiation. A seller knows a prequalified offer has a bank behind it that has already seen the numbers, and that if the deal moves forward, a carta promesa de pago will follow: the bank's written commitment to pay the seller the loan amount once the sale and the bank's mortgage are recorded at the Public Registry. Between two similar offers, the prequalified one carries more weight.

You can prequalify with more than one bank using the same set of documents, and compare.

The bank will ask every one of these questions anyway. Better to walk into the meeting with your papers in order.

Frequently asked questions

I'm selling. What should I ask of a buyer who's financing?

Their prequalification or, better, their loan approval. And make sure the purchase agreement (promesa de compraventa) says what happens if the bank turns them down or the appraisal comes in short. The loan money reaches you through the bank's carta promesa de pago, its written commitment to pay you the loan amount; the buyer's bank confirms the exact order of each step.
