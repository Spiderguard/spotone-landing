Buying is easy. Unwinding it isn't.

You can always sell. But every full round of buying and selling leaves something on the table, and that should make you less eager to rush.

Before talking about neighborhoods or budget, there's one question worth answering first: how many years do you plan to stay?

Few people have thought it through, and it matters more than anything else.

How long do you need to stay in a home in Panama to break even?

It depends on what it costs to get in and out: closing costs, taxes on the sale, months on the market, moving and repairs. That has to be covered by what the property gains and, partly, by the rent you stop paying. There's no fixed number for Panama, but buying and selling within two or three years usually loses money.

What gets left on the table

A hypothetical example. You buy at $300,000. Between the lawyer, bank and closing costs, the move and whatever you fix once you're in, $15,000 is gone.

Two years in, life takes you somewhere else. The sale takes months, and in the meantime you keep paying the condo fee, the utilities and the interest on the mortgage. When you sell, the transfer tax, which in Panama the seller pays, an advance on income tax and the cost of selling all come out of the price. If the wait and the exit take another $35,000, the property would have had to rise $50,000 in two years just to leave you where you started. One dollar in six.

And that assumes you sell at the price and on the timeline you expected. If the market is soft when you need out, the hole gets deeper, because real estate doesn't always go up. A good part of the exit costs is also calculated on the sale price, so if the property rises, they rise with it.

The example only looks at the cost of getting in and out. It doesn't subtract the rent you would have paid over those two years; that comparison is in Rent or buy? And the part people forget is time on the market: if you've already bought the next place, you're paying for two.

With a long horizon, that same cost is spread over many years and weighs little, and you give the property time to appreciate. With a short one, it eats any reasonable gain, even if the property is exactly the same.

The five-year rule you'll find online comes from the United States, built on American closing costs and American tax rules. Panama's are different, so run the numbers with Panama's.

Two kinds of decision

Some decisions are cheap to undo and some aren't. In a letter to shareholders, Bezos split his that way: the reversible ones get made fast and corrected along the way; the nearly irreversible ones get made slowly and with input from others.

Buying a home is the second kind. You can sell, of course. But every full round leaves a piece of your net worth behind, and you have to earn that piece back before you've made anything.

Rent for a year first

If you've just arrived in Panama, or you're about to change neighborhoods, renting for a year where you plan to buy is the cheapest information you can get. You see what the building is like on an ordinary Tuesday. You learn what the hallway sounds like on a Saturday.

And if after a year the area isn't for you, you move. It cost you a move. If a two- or three-year work contract brought you here, that's your horizon. To choose where to try, it helps to know that there's no best neighborhood in Panama, only the best one for you.

How to set your horizon

Two exercises, before you look at properties:

  • Write down how many years you plan to stay and what would have to happen for you to leave sooner: a transfer, a contract that isn't renewed, a child, a change of school. If two of you are buying, both of you write it down.
  • Look at each option the way the person who buys it from you will, because that person will exist. It's buying with the way out in mind. The unit most people would want in that building sells or rents with less friction if you have to leave earlier than planned.

If the horizon comes out short or fuzzy, waiting or renting almost always costs less than buying. If it comes out long and clear, buying starts to make sense, and that's when the conversation turns to the property.

And if you already bought and got the area or the building wrong, what you already spent shouldn't decide whether you stay.

Frequently asked questions

What if I buy and rent it out when I leave?

It can work, but it's a different decision. You go from an owner who lives there to an owner who manages: tenants, empty months, repairs from a distance, and the condo fee keeps running. If net rent covers the fee, the interest and upkeep, you buy time to sell when it suits you. If it doesn't, you've only postponed the exit. Do that math before you buy.
