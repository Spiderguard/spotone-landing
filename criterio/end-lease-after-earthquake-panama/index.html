Can a tenant end the lease after an earthquake?

In a residential lease, arguing over whether the earthquake is a valid cause may begin with the wrong question.

After an earthquake, a tenant may decide that they no longer want to remain in the building. The owner may answer that the lease is still in force and that the tremor does not prove the apartment is unsafe.

For a residential lease in Panama, both sides may be debating the wrong question.

The Type B Residential Lease Agreement published by Panama's Ministry of Housing and Territorial Planning —the form for monthly rents above B/.150— states that the tenant may withdraw from the lease term and its renewal at any time. The condition is written notice to the landlord at least thirty calendar days before vacating.

The clause does not require the tenant to prove an earthquake, a job transfer or another cause. It regulates the exit mechanism.

Can the tenant leave?

Under MIVIOT's residential form, the tenant may withdraw from the agreed term. But physically leaving and closing the contractual relationship properly are not the same thing.

The notice must be written and respect the required period. The form also states that a total or partial failure to give notice may result in all or part of the deposit being delivered to the landlord in proportion to the days of notice omitted.

That does not mean every deposit dispute is automatic. Unpaid rent, damage, specific contractual provisions, previous communications or competing claims may require review. Nor should a rule for residential leases be applied automatically to commercial, professional or industrial agreements.

The useful reading is straightforward:

  • the tenant should not disappear or simply return the keys;
  • the owner should not respond only that «an earthquake does not cancel a lease»;
  • both parties should organise the notice, handover date, inspection, inventory, outstanding accounts and deposit.

The earthquake changes a separate conversation

Even when the tenant does not need to invoke it as the reason for ending the term, an earthquake may cause damage that must be documented and addressed.

The Type B form requires the tenant to report damage to the property as soon as possible. It also requires the owner to keep the premises in adequate hygienic and safety conditions and to carry out the repairs needed for their intended use. Who answers for a damaged appliance is a separate discussion from ending the lease.

Separate three issues:

  • Ending the lease. Follow the applicable mechanism and notice period.
  • The condition of the property. Document it with photographs, video and written communications; obtain a qualified technical assessment when needed.
  • The deposit and outstanding obligations. Review the entry inventory, exit inspection and available evidence.

A visible crack does not allow a broker, owner or tenant to determine whether structural damage exists. That conclusion belongs to a qualified professional. If the doubt is about the building rather than the contract, it helps to understand which code it was designed under.

A practical exit record

If the tenant decides to move, the written record should cover the notice date, planned vacancy date, observed damage, requests for inspection, handover, meter readings, inventory, outstanding accounts and the deposit process.

Fear should not be minimised. It should not be turned into an improvised structural diagnosis or contractual interpretation either.

The practical question is not only whether the earthquake allows the tenant to leave. It is how the property's condition is documented and how the relationship is closed without creating another dispute.

The event may accelerate the decision. The lease still requires an orderly exit.
