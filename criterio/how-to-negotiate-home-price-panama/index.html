"Firm price" also means "I can wait"

How to make an offer without closing the door when the owner can wait longer than you can.

Message me on WhatsApp

When a listing in Panama says precio firme (firm price), it tells you less about the price than about the owner. Often it means they can wait.

That owner has no mortgage squeezing them and no moving date. Maybe the house was inherited and is paid off, maybe they rent it out in the meantime. What they have is time, and time changes what works in a negotiation.

How do you negotiate the price of a house in Panama?

Negotiating a house price in Panama with an owner in no hurry starts with understanding what they need beyond the number. The offer rests on comparables from the same model, building or area, and gets stronger with terms that suit them: handover date, form of payment, the condition you accept. Pushing them with a lowball number rarely works.

This applies to resale. A developer selling from a price list, phase by phase, is a different conversation.

Why pressure doesn't land

With an owner in a hurry, a low offer with a short deadline sometimes works. With one who can wait, it confirms that the buyer doesn't know what the house is worth, and gives them one more reason not to answer.

That holds even in a buyer's market. An owner who doesn't need to sell this year cares little about the city average. They decide on their own schedule.

The reasons that do count

The first is an honest comparison: houses of the same model, in the same development or on an equivalent street (or the same floor plan in the same building, for an apartment), with their condition and size. One that sold carries more weight than every listing still up, because a property is worth what someone pays for it.

The hard part is getting that number. In Panama, the sale price goes into the deed, but there is no public database of sales you can search easily. What you see, almost always, are asking prices. So ask your agent which sale they actually know of and where that reference comes from.

One thing you can check yourself before making an offer is the finca, the number under which the property is registered. At the Public Registry you can see who is listed as owner and whether there are mortgages or liens. If several heirs are listed, you know how many people have to say yes.

If the house has been listed for months, the owner knows. Bring it up with respect, as something they already know, never to make them feel bad.

Terms that are worth money

The other reason sits outside the price.

  • The date. Some owners need months to move out and will appreciate a flexible handover date.
  • The form of payment: cash, or a bank pre-qualification in hand, which tells the owner a lender has already looked at your numbers.
  • The condition. Taking the house as is, without asking for repairs, spares them a conversation they don't want to have. Get an inspection anyway, so you know what you're accepting.
  • What stays: furniture, appliances, the backup generator nobody wants to remove.

For an owner in no hurry, a lower price with those terms can be worth more than a higher one that complicates their life. Whatever you agree has to be written into the promesa de compraventa (the purchase agreement that sets price, deadlines and conditions before the deed), with the inventory and the dates.

Ask before you offer

The useful questions make them explain their number. Instead of "what's your lowest?", which only invites them to repeat the price, try "how did you arrive at that number?" or "what would have to happen for an offer below it to make sense to you?"

The answer shows where their real number lives. It might be a debt they want to pay off, what a neighbor says they got, or a figure someone gave them years ago. Each is handled differently, and none comes out if the conversation opens with a number.

If the answer is no

You don't have to withdraw the offer outright. Leave it in writing, with a date you'll hold it until and an open invitation to revisit it if something changes.

With these owners, what usually changes is their life: a move, an inheritance being divided, another purchase that does have a deadline.

Before you sign the promesa, have a lawyer review the terms you negotiated.

Frequently asked questions

How far below the asking price can I offer?

There is no percentage that works for every property. The room you have depends on the comparables and on what this home has or lacks next to them. A low offer with reasons opens a conversation. Without reasons, the owner usually won't even reply.
